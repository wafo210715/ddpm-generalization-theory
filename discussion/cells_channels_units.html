1How big is a zoom-15 tile

Web Mercator tiles shrink with latitude. Width in metres is 40,075,017 × cos(latitude) ÷ 2zoom. Ho Chi Minh City spans 10.35° N at Cần Giờ to 11.16° N at Củ Chi, so every tile in the study area is within a quarter of a percent of the same size.

A standard slippy-map tile image is 256 × 256 pixels. That is a rendering convention for web maps and says nothing about our raster. The resolution inside the tile is ours to choose. At zoom 15, each choice of image size fixes the unit, and the cost follows the pixel count:

2The unit is free above a floor

Your insertion is right, with one boundary. An indicator has two scales. The measurement scale is where it is computed: the decay radius of the gravity model, the ring around a point for POI diversity, the polygon itself for a footprint. The sampling unit is where its value is read: the pixel. Reading a smooth field at more points does not change what the field means. It only records more samples of the same thing.

The boundary is the finest indicator. Ho Chi Minh City's rowhouses are 4 to 6 m wide and its alleys 2 to 4 m. Pixels of 10 m fuse a row of houses and their alleys into one block; pixels of 2 to 3 m keep them apart. Accessibility and diversity are smooth by construction and would be fine at 25 or 50 m, but every channel shares one grid, so the building channel sets the floor for all of them.

Decision. 1024 px at zoom 15, a 1.17 m unit. Your call, given HCMC's ground conditions: at that size a 3 m alley is two to three pixels wide and a 5 m rowhouse keeps its outline. 512 px, at 2.35 m, is the first size that respects the footprints and serves as the calibration tier. Build and debug at 128 or 256 px, calibrate at 512, run the experiments at 1024.

Three things 1024 px commits you to. The standard DDPM noise schedule under-noises large images, so it must be shifted, and the U-Net needs more downsampling levels so that its attention block sees a small bottleneck; Simple Diffusion (Hoogeboom et al. 2023) trained 1024 px in pixel space with exactly these two changes, and Chen 2023 studies the schedule shift on its own. Compute per step is 64 times the pilot's, so a 500K-step run is weeks by naive scaling; smaller mutants reach their memorization onset sooner and need fewer steps, which helps. And nearest-neighbour distances get noisier in a space of six million numbers per cell, although the intrinsic dimension itself does not change with resolution (Haldar et al. 2024, already cited in the June plan).

3What a channel is

A cell is a stack of grids. Each grid holds one indicator's score for every unit in the cell. Six indicators, six grids. A single "pixel" is not a colour: it is the column of six scores that one patch of urban space receives. The pilot's RGB files were exactly this object with three grids, stored in an image format because the loader wanted one. The colours were never colours, and nothing about them was wasted or special.

So more channels are not wasteful, and three is not special. The U-Net takes any channel count, one included: the MNIST baseline in the same code trained on a single grid. The only things that change with the count are the loader, the per-channel standardization, and how you look at the result.

The same holds if the vitality literature hands you fourteen indicators. Fourteen grids at 1024 px are fourteen million numbers per cell, which the model does not mind: the first convolution absorbs any channel count, standardization gives each grid equal weight, and the intrinsic dimension barely moves because most published indicators are functions of the same underlying city. What changes is the work in the specification table. Each candidate gets sorted three ways. A field that varies within a tile becomes a grid. A value that is constant across a tile, which many published indicators are, becomes a condition attached to the cell. A near-duplicate of an indicator already present is dropped, and the correlationist baseline is where that shows. Expect fourteen candidates to become five to eight grids and a few conditions.

4Unpacking a sample

The model generates a tensor of the same shape as the training tensors. Unpacking is mechanical: split off each grid and undo its standardization with the shift and scale stored at encoding time. That returns physical units, such as footprint area per unit or bits of POI entropy. Only then does the descriptor run, and it runs identically on real and generated cells. So yes, the descriptor is the comparison tool, and unpacking is the free step before it.

Your thought experiment with six numbers per data point is the descriptor-level version of this. It is a legitimate and cheap baseline: a DDPM on per-cell six-vectors learns the joint distribution of the indicators without their spatial arrangement. The raster version learns that plus layout. Running both tells you how much layout matters, which is itself a finding.

5Seeing the change after training

Your idea, in two parts. First, flatten cells onto a two-dimensional map and watch where generated cells land relative to real ones, one grid at a time. Second, measure how the grids sit relative to each other in space, before and after training, so the change reads from what to what. Both were missing from the map; they are now C4b, E3b and E3c.

The map. Flatten each cell first to about fifty numbers with PCA, then to two with UMAP or t-SNE, fitted on the real cells only and before any model exists. That is the before picture, C4b, and it also shows whether the typologies separate. After training, project generated cells onto the same map, one grid at a time and one checkpoint at a time, E3b. Three patterns matter. Generated points that fill a real cluster mean that region was learned. A real cluster with no generated points near it was not. Generated points piled onto a single real cell are copying, which F_mem then confirms in numbers. Use UMAP rather than t-SNE for this, because UMAP can place new points on a map fitted earlier and t-SNE has to be refitted every time. Treat the picture as a picture: distances on it are not measurements.

The spatial relations. The pilot's synergy analysis correlated cell-level averages, which can say that dense cells tend to be accessible cells but not whether, inside a cell, the buildings sit on the accessible units. That is a question about two grids at once in space. Bivariate Moran's I answers it directly; co-location counts between unit classes and cross-correlograms by distance add detail. Compute them once on the real cells and at every checkpoint on generated cells, E3c, and the change reads as a number moving from one value to another over training.

6Supply and demand in one tensor

Putting form and activity into one tensor is not a category error. It is the point. A regression fits one function from supply to demand. The DDPM learns the joint distribution of both, and from a joint you can read either conditional: what activity accompanies a given form, or what forms host a given activity. Fixing the form grids and sampling only the activity grids, the inpainting trick, is how a trained model answers the first question directly. That is the intervention experiment your Stage 2 plan wanted, without a new architecture.

So the caution before adding activity layers is practical, not conceptual. VIIRS night lights come at about 500 m. A zoom-15 tile is 2.4 of those cells wide, so within a tile the layer is nearly constant. Either treat coarse layers as conditions attached to the whole cell, or look for activity proxies at building scale, such as POI visit counts or mobile-signal grids where they exist.

7Raster stack or direct encoding

Your list is right: direct encoding needs a far more complex encoder, is less legible to urban readers, and drops the forced grid in favour of georeferencing. It also changes one thing you did not list. In the raster stack, the way back from a sample to indicators is free, because the encoding was a written rule. In direct encoding, the way back is a learned decoder that must itself be trained and validated, one per data type. That is the hardest part of the Stage 2 plan. It is also why JEPA-style representation learning is not a shortcut here: JEPA learns embeddings to predict with, not a space you can sample from and decode.

The generalization theory you rely on is also defined in data space. A near-copy in a raster is unambiguous; a near-copy in a learned embedding depends on what the encoder chose to keep. Which is why, as you said, the generative model should at least generate something you can unpack and look at. Path one gives you that for free.

Terms in plain words

Cell
One map tile. One training example. Everything the model sees at once.
Unit
One pixel inside a cell: the patch of urban space that each indicator gives a score to.
Channel
One indicator's grid of unit scores for a cell. Six indicators make six greyscale grids. The pilot's RGB file was three of these in an image container; the colours were never colours.
Tensor
The whole stack of grids for one cell. What the model learns from and what it generates.
Standardization
Shifting and scaling each channel so its values centre on zero with a spread of one. The shift and scale are stored, so the step can be undone on a generated sample.
Descriptor
A fixed recipe that turns one cell into a short list of numbers: per-channel average, spread, share of empty units, spatial clumping, class mix. Run on real and generated cells alike, then the two lists of numbers are compared.
F_mem, memorization fraction, k-NN gap ratio
The share of generated cells that are near-copies of a single training cell. Found by the gap ratio: distance to the nearest training cell divided by distance to the second nearest. A ratio below one third means the sample sits on top of one training cell and counts as a copy.
Noise floor
What F_mem reads on a model with random, never-trained weights. Only values above it mean the trained model actually copied.
Generalization window
The stretch of training after the model has learned the structure of the data and before it starts copying individual cells.
Generalization theory
Bonnaire et al.'s account of diffusion training: the model first learns the shared structure of the data and only later starts copying individual cells. The window between the two is what we study; memorization is its far edge.
Memorization onset, τ_mem
How many training steps pass before copying starts. Generalization theory shows it grows in proportion to the number of training cells, so bigger datasets stay in the window longer.
Intrinsic dimension
How many independent knobs it takes to describe the dataset, even though each cell holds millions of numbers. The pilot estimated about 33 by PCA.
PCA k95
The number of straight-line directions needed to explain 95 percent of the dataset's spread. Overestimates when the data is curved.
Levina-Bickel MLE
An estimate of intrinsic dimension from how nearest-neighbour distances grow with rank. It follows curvature, so it usually gives a smaller number than PCA.
Typology
A group of cells that look alike, found by clustering. The building blocks for dataset mutants.
HDBSCAN
A clustering method that finds groups of any shape, leaves stragglers unassigned, and copes with dense and sparse regions at the same time. Needs no preset number of clusters.
Subspace
A flat, low-dimensional sheet inside the big space of all possible cells. Wang et al. assume each typology lies on its own sheet.
k-center greedy
A way to pick a small set of cells that spread out over the whole dataset: repeatedly add the cell farthest from those already chosen.
Mutant
A training dataset made from the baseline by adding or removing whole typologies. Comparing models trained on mutants shows which structures survive.
Moran's I
A number for spatial clumping. Near 1, neighbouring units look alike; near 0, there is no spatial pattern at all.
Wasserstein distance
How much material you would have to move to turn one histogram into another. A distance between two distributions.
Jacobian rank
How many directions the trained denoiser actually responds to. A low rank means it has learned a low-dimensional structure rather than reacting to every pixel independently.
Hutchinson estimator
A trick to estimate that rank with random probes instead of computing the full matrix, which would be far too large.
UMAP and t-SNE
Two ways to flatten many-number cells onto a 2-D map so that cells which resemble each other land close together. Good for seeing clusters and gaps, bad for measuring: distances on the map are not distances in the data. UMAP can place new points on a map fitted earlier; t-SNE must be refitted from scratch.
Bivariate Moran's I
Moran's I between two grids: whether high values of one indicator sit next to high values of another in space. Near 1, buildings cluster around accessible units; near 0, the two grids ignore each other spatially.
Small multiples
Many small panels with the same layout side by side, one per channel, so the eye compares them.
False-colour composite
Showing any three channels as red, green and blue. What remote sensing does with satellite bands.
