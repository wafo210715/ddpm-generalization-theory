Why one unit, and why whole tiles

The unit belongs to the grid, not to the thing being drawn. An earlier version fitted each area's bounding box to a fixed image width, so the metres per pixel came out different for every district — 1.68 in one, 3.44 in another. That makes two images incomparable even though they show the same layers. Here every ward is 1 201 m ÷ 320 px, so the same distance is the same number of pixels everywhere in the city.

A ward is not what the model sees; a tile is. So each render is the square block of tiles covering the ward, padded out with real neighbouring tiles rather than blank space. The ward crosses tile boundaries, several tiles are shared between wards, and both facts are visible rather than hidden by a crop.

What to look for. Footprints should show the plot-and-alley grain rather than blocks. Height should pick out the towers and leave the rowhouse fabric flat. Place density should concentrate on the markets and the commercial streets. If a layer looks like noise where you know the ground is structured, that is a data problem worth finding now rather than after a training run.

Buildings and places from Overture; heights from 3D-GloBFP; roads, transit, water and green from OpenStreetMap; vegetation from WorldCover; population from WorldPop. Reproduce: scripts/render_wards.py
