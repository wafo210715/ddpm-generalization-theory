## 2.2 The Limits of the "Correlationist" Paradigm in Urban Vitality

Drawing on [Huang - 2025]'s theoretical framework, we can identify an analogous epistemological distinction within urban vitality research. Huang argues that architectural intelligence is undergoing a transition from symbolic rationality — where design knowledge is codified through predefined rules, fixed typologies, and expert-defined geometric schemata — to connectionist rationality, where knowledge is learned directly from data through probabilistic inference, and form emerges from the navigation of latent probability fields rather than the application of explicit rules. We argue that urban vitality research has arrived at a structurally similar juncture, though expressed through different methodological apparatus. What we term the "correlationist" paradigm — an adaptation of Huang's symbolic mode to the specific context of urban vitality studies — operates through the extraction of statistical associations between predefined variables, producing knowledge in the form of feature weights, regression coefficients, and ranked factor importance. The connectionist alternative, by contrast, would learn the geometry of the underlying urban data manifold through probabilistic inference, capturing the joint distribution of urban variables rather than isolating their individual contributions. This distinction provides the analytical lens for the critique that follows.

Contemporary urban vitality research operates squarely within what we have termed the correlationist mode. Driven by the proliferation of multi-source big data, the field has achieved unprecedented quantification of urban phenomena but has simultaneously reinforced a paradigm that prioritizes statistical associations over generative mechanisms. Vitality is operationalized through digital traces: Location-based services and mobile signaling data capture human mobility ([Kim - 2020]; [Li et al. - 2022]; [Liu et al. - 2019]), social media check-ins indicate economic and cultural engagement ([Liu et al. - 2022]; [Qin et al. - 2025]; [Scepanovic et al. - 2021]), and Point of Interest density metrics quantify functional intensity ([Jiang et al. - 2024]; [Wu et al. - 2023]). Researchers employ a standard toolkit — OLS, spatial regression, GWR, and machine learning algorithms like Random Forest ([Jiang et al. - 2024]; [Li et al. - 2022]; [Wu et al. - 2023]) — to catalogue positive and negative drivers of vitality ([Wu et al. - 2023]; [Xia et al. - 2020]; [Zeng et al. - 2018]). While this quantification has enabled analyses ranging from street-level grids to city-wide assessments ([Pan et al. - 2021]; [Xia et al. - 2020]), it often results in "conceptual reductionism," where complex social interactions and experiential dimensions are flattened into digital signals ([Doan and Zhang - 2025]). As Section 2.1 demonstrated, these proxies not only reduce complexity but conflate structurally distinct supply and demand dimensions within the very vitality measures they construct.

However, this correlationist approach encounters an epistemic wall defined by two fundamental and interrelated limitations. First, it relies on an additivity assumption that treats urban factors as independent, linear contributors to vitality — failing to account for the non-linear dynamics, including interaction effects, synergies, and threshold behaviors, that characterize complex urban systems. As [Doan and Zhang - 2025] document, prevailing methods "operate under the assumptions of linearity, additivity, and homoscedasticity," assumptions that, as [Wu et al. - 2023] warn, produce "erroneous conclusions" when applied to systems where non-linear relationships are the norm. The practical consequence is not merely imprecision but systematic blindness to critical patterns: [Tan et al. - 2026], deploying the most comprehensive multi-method response to date (Random Forest with SHAP explanations, Partial Dependence Plots, Interpretative Structural Modeling, and Bayesian Networks), demonstrate that Floor Area Ratio must exceed approximately 0.25 before complementary amenities generate significant vitality gains, while food services density exhibits a negative association beyond saturation — threshold dynamics that render linear coefficients qualitatively misleading. Second, the paradigm suffers from context stripping along two axes. Statistically, urban variables are analyzed in isolation — density, accessibility, and diversity are treated as independent columns rather than co-occurring features of the same urban configuration, losing the relational structure of how these attributes jointly distribute across the city. Spatially, geographic units are treated as isolated observations, severing the cross-boundary interdependencies that shape lived urban experience. Both dimensions are empirically consequential. Spatial econometric studies have established that vitality exhibits significant spatial autocorrelation, violating the independence assumptions of standard models ([Jiang et al. - 2024]; [Li et al. - 2022]), and that cross-boundary spillover effects reveal dynamics invisible to aspatial specifications — including, as [Collins et al. - 2024] demonstrate, gender-differentiated externalities where built environment improvements in one district generate vibrancy benefits in neighboring areas that accrue differently to men and women. Together, these limitations — the inability to capture non-linear joint effects and the severing of both statistical and spatial context — mean that the correlationist paradigm structurally cannot access the emergent properties that foundational theorists identify as the essence of urban vitality.

In sum, the correlationist paradigm generates actionable feature lists but offers limited insight into the joint probability distributions and generative processes that produce vibrant urban environments. This creates a fundamental epistemological dissonance with the theoretical roots established in Section 2.1: Jacobs, Landry, and Montgomery define vitality as an emergent property — a "sense of place" arising from the dynamic interplay of social, economic, and physical elements — while the correlationist paradigm attempts to predict this emergence using linear, additive drivers. It is this failure to transcend the predict-and-optimize paradigm — even within computational methods — that necessitates a shift toward connectionist modeling, where urban vitality is reconceived as an adaptive capacity grounded in probabilistic inference rather than explicit rule optimization.
