# 1. Introduction

The proliferation of multi-source urban big data has fundamentally altered the landscape of urban vitality research. What was once a qualitative inquiry into the social life of small urban spaces has evolved into a rigorous science of quantification, where location-based services, social media data, and built-environment metrics are routinely synthesized to map the pulse of the city (Kim, 2020; X. Li et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2019). Yet, despite this empirical expansion, the field remains theoretically constrained by a "correlationist" paradigm. Dominant methodologies, ranging from spatial regression to random forest algorithms, prioritize the identification of statistical associations over the explication of generative mechanisms (Jiang et al., 2024; C. Wu et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2018). Within this framework, urban vitality is treated as a dependent variable to be predicted by independent factors, effectively flattening the complex, emergent synergies of the city into linear, additive lists of drivers (X. Li et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2021). As recent systematic reviews indicate, this approach frequently suffers from "conceptual reductionism," where the lived experience of the city is severed from its context and reduced to isolated digital signals (Doan & Zhang, 2025).

This study argues that the true significance of contemporary generative AI — specifically Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) — lies not in its ability to automate urban design or visualize "future cities" (He et al., 2026; Q. Wang et al., 2025; Zhuang et al., 2024), but in its potential to function as an epistemic probe into this complexity. Unlike the correlationist approach, which strips context to isolate variables, diffusion models operate as "structure-preserving compressors" (P. Wang et al., 2025). By learning to reconstruct urban data from noise, these models do not merely memorize pixels; they internalize the low-dimensional manifold of the city — the hidden, intrinsic constraints and joint probability distributions that govern how physical form and social activity coalesce into vibrant environments (P. Wang et al., 2025).

From this perspective, the training of a diffusion model is not a technical task of image synthesis, but a methodological experiment in manifold learning. If, as recent computer science literature suggests, diffusion models exhibit a distinct "generalization window" where structural concepts are learned before noisy details (Bonnaire et al., 2025), then the training process itself offers a window into the structural essence of urban form. By observing what the model prioritizes and preserves during this window, we can distinguish between fundamental, irreducible structural patterns and the incidental noise of specific locales.

However, a critical gap separates these theoretical properties from their application to urban data. The evidence for the manifold-learning behavior of diffusion models derives almost exclusively from controlled computational settings. Bonnaire et al. (2025) validate the generalization window on synthetic mixture-of-Gaussians distributions; Wang et al. (2025) demonstrate the subspace clustering equivalence on CIFAR-10 (32×32 pixels) and CelebA (64×64 pixels); Kadkhodaie et al. (2024) establish geometry-adaptive harmonic representations on similarly low-resolution benchmark datasets. These datasets share three characteristics that urban geospatial data emphatically do not: low resolution, statistical regularity, and homogeneity. Urban data — encoded through the lossy compression of rasterization from heterogeneous geospatial sources (polygons, polylines, points) — is high-dimensional, structurally irregular, and devoid of ground-truth labels. Whether the theoretical properties of DDPMs — the generalization window, the structure-preserving compression, the subspace clustering equivalence — survive contact with such data is an open empirical question. Answering it is the methodological significance of this paper.

To bridge this gap, we require a data encoding strategy that preserves heterogeneity without imposing the very assumptions the correlationist paradigm carries. We propose RGB channel synthesis, a technique that maps distinct rasterized urban variables — building density (R), road accessibility (G), and POI diversity (B) — into separate color channels (X. Liu et al., 2024). This approach makes no composite vitality assumptions: no pre-calculated scores, no variable weighting, no human annotation. The raw, unweighted relationships between urban variables are preserved, and the model is forced to learn their joint structure rather than receiving it pre-digested through preprocessing. It should be noted that this study addresses the supply side of the built environment only; the integration of demand-side human activity data remains future work. This framing yields a single research question:

**Can a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model learn the low-dimensional manifold of urban built environment from RGB-encoded geospatial data?**

This question is examined through three analytical dimensions: (1) whether a generalization window emerges during training on urban data; (2) what structural patterns persist during this window; and (3) how the distributions of generated samples compare to those of the training data.

This paper offers two layers of significance. Methodologically, if a DDPM can learn a meaningful manifold from urban geospatial data, it validates the transferability of generalization theory from controlled computational experiments to complex real-world spatial data, and demonstrates that the structure-preserving compressor property holds beyond benchmark datasets — establishing a new analytical pathway beyond the correlationist paradigm. Epistemologically, regardless of the specific experimental outcome, the framework itself offers a new way to conceptualize urban vitality research. By framing the question through the supply-demand lens and treating the model as an epistemic probe, the analytical orientation shifts from "which factors predict vitality?" to "what structure underlies the built environment?" — a fundamentally different epistemological position. This paper is primarily a methods paper: testing feasibility, documenting the theory-to-practice gap, and proposing a framework for future development.

The scope of this study is explicitly bounded. The study area is a single city (Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam). The data comprise built-environment supply-side variables only (building footprint, road networks, POIs). The analysis is preliminary — training dynamics and initial sampling — rather than a full characterization of the learned manifold. Multi-city comparison, supply-demand joint learning, and systematic data composition experiments are deferred to future work. The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows: Section 2 reviews urban vitality theory and DDPM properties; Section 3 details the methodology; Section 4 presents preliminary experiments; Section 5 discusses findings, limitations, and future directions.
