## 2.3 Diffusion Models: From Image Synthesis to Manifold Learning

The emergence of pixel-space DDPMs offers a theoretical pathway beyond correlation, shifting focus from deterministic mapping to stochastic manifold learning. Unlike traditional generative models, DDPMs operate through probabilistic inference, reversing a diffusion process to learn the gradient of the data's log probability density directly in the high-dimensional pixel space ([Wang et al. - 2025 - Diffusion Models Learn Low-Dimensional]). This distinction matters because generalization theory is substantially more mature for U-Net-based pixel-space diffusion than for VAE-based latent diffusion models. In pixel-space DDPMs, the learned score function corresponds to the true data distribution gradient, enabling rigorous analysis of how the model captures the data manifold's geometry ([Song et al. - 2021]). Latent diffusion models, while computationally efficient, introduce a compression bottleneck through the VAE encoder-decoder, complicating theoretical guarantees about what structure is preserved in the learned representation ([Rombach et al. - 2022]). For probing urban structure at 128×128 resolution—a scale where computational cost is manageable—theoretical clarity outweighs the efficiency gains of latent space methods. This mechanism is underpinned by the Manifold Hypothesis, which posits that high-dimensional data resides on low-dimensional manifolds governed by intrinsic, albeit often unobservable, constraints. In classical learning theory, accurately capturing this underlying distribution is the prerequisite for generalization ([Vapnik - 2000]). Diffusion models succeed not by memorizing pixel-level correlations, but by learning the geometry of this manifold. As [Wang et al. - 2025 - Diffusion Models Learn Low-Dimensional] demonstrate, optimizing diffusion training is mathematically equivalent to solving a canonical subspace clustering problem — an unsupervised method for identifying and grouping data points that lie on low-dimensional subspaces within high-dimensional space. Crucially, the learned subspace basis vectors align with "semantically meaningful directions," enabling controllable generation of attributes such as gender, hairstyle, and color without additional training. This suggests that diffusion models do not merely replicate training instances but discover meaningful structural sub-organizations in the data ([Kadkhodaie 等 - 2024]). Furthermore, [Zhang et al. - 2024] demonstrate that diffusion models exhibit a unique property of "model reproducibility": different model architectures, trained on non-overlapping data subsets, converge to consistent generated outputs when given the same noise input — implying that all models learn the same score function, capturing robust generative laws rather than dataset-specific artifacts.

Two theoretical properties make diffusion models particularly potent for urban analysis. First, they fundamentally address the "curse of dimensionality." [Wang et al. - 2025 - Diffusion Models Learn Low-Dimensional] demonstrate that the mathematical equivalence between diffusion training and subspace clustering means the model automatically identifies the intrinsic dimensionality of the data. This aligns with findings by [Cole & Lu - 2024] that score-based models can learn distributions with sample complexities scaling linearly with the data's intrinsic dimension — suggesting that even with minimal datasets that meet the intrinsic dimensional threshold, the model can organize data into meaningful regimes without human supervision.

Second, and most critical for our hypothesis, is the phenomenon of implicit dynamical regularization. While large models are capable of a "memorization regime" — learning the empirical score to perfectly reproduce training data — they do not start there ([Biroli et al. - 2024]; [Li et al. - 2024]). Instead, [Bonnaire et al. - 2025] identify a distinct "generalization window": a training phase where the model generates structured, generalizable outputs before transitioning to memorization. This behavior is quantified through the f_mem metric, which measures the fraction of generated samples whose nearest-neighbor distance ratio falls below a threshold, distinguishing memorized outputs from generalized ones. The generalization window is driven by "spectral bias," where neural networks learn low-frequency, structural patterns significantly faster than high-frequency noise ([Rahaman et al. - 2019]; [Wang & Pehlevan - 2025]). It should be noted that these theoretical properties have been validated primarily on controlled datasets: [Bonnaire et al. - 2025] on CelebA (32×32 grayscale, downsampled) and synthetic Gaussian mixtures; [Wang et al. - 2025 - Diffusion Models Learn Low-Dimensional] on MNIST, FashionMNIST, CIFAR-10, FFHQ, CelebA, and AFHQ; [Zhang et al. - 2024] primarily on CIFAR-10. Whether these properties transfer to complex, high-dimensional urban geospatial data remains an open question — the central empirical concern of this paper.

This provides a crucial methodological opening for urban analysis. Because the internal logic of the model's learned representation remains opaque, we cannot directly observe how it encodes urban structure. However, we can probe the model's behavior through its inputs and outputs. By sampling from the model at checkpoints throughout training — particularly during the generalization window — and analyzing the stability of generated forms, we can infer the structural essence of the urban built environment: features that remain constant during this window represent the learned structural signal, while those that appear only later represent memorized noise ([Wu et al. - 2025]). This probing can be operationalized through nearest-neighbor analysis between generated and training samples, and through RGB channel decomposition of generated images to examine what the model has learned along each urban dimension. This approach reframes the analytical question: rather than asking "which factors predict vitality?", we ask "what structure does the model internalize as essential?"

Despite this potential, current applications of generative models in urban planning largely treat them as black box design generators. A systematic review of 120 generative urban design papers by [Jiang 等 - 2024] found that the field exclusively frames generative models as tools for automated design synthesis and visualization, with no discussion of analytical or epistemic uses. This is exemplified by recent "Text-to-City" frameworks that utilize diffusion models primarily for generating urban form from text prompts ([He et al. - 2026]; [Wang et al. - 2025] (Q. Wang); [Zhuang et al. - 2024]). These frameworks typically employ latent diffusion models (LDMs) for computational efficiency, trading theoretical clarity for faster training and generation at higher resolutions. While LDMs succeed as design tools, the VAE bottleneck obscures the relationship between learned latent representations and the original pixel-space data manifold ([Rombach et al. - 2022]). These studies focus on instrumental metrics of visual fidelity, inadvertently pushing models into the memorization regime where they risk becoming engines of "digital forgery" ([Carlini et al. - 2023]; [Somepalli et al. - 2022]). By treating the model solely as a drafting tool, the field neglects its internal clustering and generalization behaviors ([George et al. - 2025]). Even more structurally focused approaches, such as GNN-based models like GlobalMapper and COHO ([He & Aliaga, 2023, 2024]), which demonstrate that learned latent representations can predict local socio-economic metrics with over 75% accuracy, treat the model's analytical capacity as a byproduct of design generation rather than as the primary object of inquiry. Consequently, the opportunity to use generative models as computational laboratories for identifying the hidden structures of the city remains largely unexplored.
