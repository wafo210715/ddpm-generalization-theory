## 2.1 From Jacobs to Quantification: The Conceptual Evolution of Urban Vitality

Urban vitality research traces its theoretical roots to Jane Jacobs' *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961), whose insights were grounded in direct observation and a normative vision of inclusive, active urban life (Doan & Zhang, 2025; Dogan & Lee, 2024; Mouratidis & Poortinga, 2020). Jacobs sought to answer how cities work in real life — what principles of planning promote social and economic vitality, and what deadens these attributes. Her answer comprised four generators of diversity: mixed primary uses, short blocks, aged buildings, and concentration — tangible carriers through which her normative principles could be observed and assessed. As the systematic review by Doan and Zhang (2025) traces, this conceptual foundation evolved through several expansions: Whyte (1980) on activity density in public spaces; Lynch (1981) on city form as an organism whose vitality encompasses material quality, safety, human health, and ecosystem stability; Maas (1984) introducing social heterogeneity as a vitality dimension; and Montgomery (1998) drawing on sociological insights into place attachment to portray vitality as an emergent property — a "sense of place" or "rhythm of life force" — arising once form, activity, and image converge. Both Jacobs and Lynch assigned urban vitality a tangible carrier (generators, morphology), while Maas and Montgomery shifted toward viewing vitality as an emergent, unobservable quality. Landry (2000, 2009) subsequently proposed a holistic view where vitality emerges from the dynamic interplay of three core elements — social, economic, and physical — each reinforcing the other to create thriving urban systems. Doan and Zhang's (2025) review documents how these foundational visions, portraying vitality as emergent and multidimensional, progressively encountered the pressure of operationalization: how does one measure a "sense of place"? The progression from tangible carriers to emergent properties represents not merely conceptual evolution but an increasing tension between vitality's theoretical richness and its resistance to direct measurement.

While these foundational visions portray vitality as a dynamic, emergent phenomenon, they remain inherently unobservable and resistant to direct measurement. The advent of big data from sensing technologies — mobile phone records, social media check-ins, POI datasets — offered unprecedented insight into population concentration and urban activity patterns, revealing dimensions of urban life that were previously inaccessible to systematic inquiry (Kim, 2020; X. Li et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2019). This empirical expansion enabled two dominant proxy strategies. On one side, human activity intensity — population concentration, mobility flows, social media engagement — captures where vitality manifests, serving as what we term the *demand* dimension: *where* does urban life concentrate? (X. Li et al., 2022; H. Liu et al., 2022; Scepanovic et al., 2021). On the other, built environment (BE) factors — density, functional mix, morphology, accessibility — capture the physical preconditions that enable vitality, serving as the *supply* dimension: *why* do certain places support vibrant life? (Chen et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2024; C. Wu et al., 2023). We propose that these two dimensions are not merely complementary but structurally distinct: the supply side represents what the physical environment *affords*, while the demand side represents what people *actually do*. Vitality emerges from their interaction, not from either alone. Importantly, the supply and demand dimensions are not symmetrical: BE factors explain *why* certain configurations support vitality, while human factors indicate *where* it manifests. Scholars correlated these proxies to quantitatively validate Jacobs et al.'s qualitative insights, birthing the correlationist paradigm — an approach that, we argue, carries within it a fundamental conflation.

Subsequent research formalized these proxies into multidimensional indices, commonly structured as a triad of density, diversity, and accessibility/connectivity — directly adapting Jacobs' generators alongside Lynch and Montgomery's insights (Gómez-Varo et al., 2022; Kim, 2020; M. Wang et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2018). This progression yielded refined frameworks such as H. Liu et al.'s (2022) "growth, diversity, mobility" and Doan and Zhang's (2025) "density, diversity, connectivity." However, the critical issue is not the construction of indices per se but how they are deployed in driving factors analysis — regression-based studies that use a vitality proxy as the dependent variable (DV) while including supply-side indicators as independent variables (IVs). Across prevailing studies, BE (supply) and human activity (demand) indicators are conflated within the DV construction itself. This is not a problem of comprehensive measurement — indices that integrate multiple dimensions are not inherently flawed — but of circular reasoning: when supply-side elements appear in both the DV and IVs, the regression effectively tests whether supply predicts supply, rendering causal interpretation invalid.

Three examples illustrate this conflation at increasing levels of subtlety. First, Li et al. (2021) construct a cultural vitality DV using cultural POI counts within 300m buffers, then regress it against IVs that include overall POI density and POI mixture entropy. This is effectively regressing POI against POI — the regression coefficient reflects spatial autocorrelation of POI data, not a causal relationship between built environment and human activity. Second, Li et al. (2022) construct a composite vitality DV from street-view imagery (pedestrians, bicycles, motor vehicles, buses, private cars, trucks) averaged with Weibo check-ins, then regress against BE supply factors including road density, shopping density, and transit facility density. The circularity here is more subtle: SVI-detected vehicles and buses represent both demand (people using transport) and supply (infrastructure presence). If bus count partially reflects transit infrastructure density — which also appears as an IV — the vitality score is inflated by supply-side visibility rather than human activity intensity. Third, Pan et al. (2021) employ PLS-SEM with a UV latent variable measured by nighttime light intensity (NTL, demand-side) and POI count (supply-side). When modeling pathways from environmental and economic variables to UV, the POI component in the latent variable means that a pathway such as "road density → UV" may operate via road density → more POIs → higher UV (a pure supply transmission) rather than road density → more human activity → higher UV (a demand effect). These two mechanisms are statistically indistinguishable in the model.

This conflation is not limited to individual studies. As a review of Chinese-language urban vitality research notes, "some studies used POI data as the representation of urban vitality while some others use POI data as an explanatory variable for modelling urban vitality" — the same data type arbitrarily switches between supply and demand roles across studies, rendering coefficients incomparable and causal pathways uninterpretable. Even ostensibly demand-side proxies suffer from hidden supply contamination. Nighttime light (NTL) data, widely used as an economic vitality proxy, reflects not only human economic activity but also electrical infrastructure coverage and streetlight density. When NTL serves as DV in BE regression, coefficients may capture "infrastructure quality → NTL intensity" rather than "human activity → NTL intensity" — a conflation invisible in standard regression diagnostics. The practical consequence is validated by Williams et al. (2019), who identified China's "ghost cities" precisely by decoupling amenity supply from behavioral demand — a distinction that single-index approaches inherently obscure. Where composite vitality scores indicated thriving districts, the supply-demand decoupling revealed empty apartment blocks surrounded by completed infrastructure: supply without demand. While the critique above targets the construction of vitality proxies, it is sustained by the statistical architectures that analyze them — creating a methodological bottleneck that further distances measurement from the generative reality of urban vitality.
